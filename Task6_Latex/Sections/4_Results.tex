\section{Results}

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimisation results}

\subsubsection{Convergence}

To better characterise the optimisation dynamics, the convergence histories are analysed in pairs. All curves exhibit the typical behaviour of PSO, with a rapid initial decay followed by a gradual stabilisation once the swarm approaches a locally optimal region. However, the rate of decrease, the number of iterations required to reach the plateau, and the presence of intermittent stagnation phases vary significantly from one case to another.

Both cases 1 and 2 show a steep cost reduction during the first iterations, dropping several orders of magnitude within the first 20 to 40 iterations. In case 1, the convergence then continues more progressively until 150 iterations, before entering a stable regime where the cost remains essentially constant for the remainder of the run. Case 2 reaches its stabilisation region sooner, around 30 iterations, after which the curve remains flat with only minor adjustments.

In contrast, case 4 stabilises earlier: after a rapid descent during the first 100 iterations, the improvement rate decreases significantly, and the plateau phase is reached around iteration 200. \subsubsection{Profiles}

Each shape reflects the balance between aerodynamic, structural, and volumetric constraints imposed during optimisation, leading to characteristic variations in tower height, waist radius, and global curvature. Grouping the cases by geometric similarity therefore facilitates a more meaningful comparison of the resulting cooling‑tower profiles.

    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.5\linewidth}

        \caption{Case 3 profile}

    \end{subfigure}\hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.5\linewidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../Images/PSO/Case6_Pso_profile.png}

        \label{fig:profpso06}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Optimised tower profiles (Cases 3 and 6)}
    \label{fig:pso-convergences_36}
\end{figure}

Cases 3 and 6 represent the most vertically developed cooling‑tower shapes within the dataset. Both exhibit a pronounced waist situated around mid‑height, followed by a strong radial expansion toward the top section—typical of tall hyperbolic structures. Case 3, however, extends significantly higher, producing a much more elongated upper region. Case 6 retains the same overall logic but delivers a more compact geometry, with smoother transitions between the waist and the upper flare. In both cases, the optimisation markedly reshapes the initial guess, steepening the curvature near the waist to accommodate the required volume. Overall, the two profiles share the same hyperbolic identity, but case 3 emphasises vertical stretching, whereas case 6 achieves a more proportionate distribution of curvature.

Cases 4 and 7 correspond to cooling‑tower geometries with medium height and a clearly defined waist. Both exhibit the classical hourglass form, with balanced radial expansion above and below the constriction. Case 4 presents a slightly broader upper flare, giving the tower a wider top contour, whereas case 7 adopts a more uniform slope, producing a narrower appearance near the upper section. The optimisation smooths the lower curvature in both cases, reducing asymmetries present in the initial guesses. Despite these subtle differences, the two profiles remain closely aligned and belong to the same geometric family, distinguished by their moderate height and regular hyperbolic curvature.

In both cases, the optimisation induces only small deviations from the initial guess, suggesting that the target geometries are already well approximated prior to refinement. These two cases form a distinct family, contrasting strongly with the tall, narrow‑waist towers and illustrating a different structural regime where the volumetric constraint promotes larger mid‑height openings.

The performance comparison reveals a clear hierarchy in computational efficiency:

\textbf{BFGS is the fastest method by a wide margin}, averaging 0.39\,s per case compared to 3.56\,s for SA and 6.04\,s for PSO. This advantage stems from the superlinear convergence rate of quasi-Newton methods: BFGS requires on average only 5\,256 function evaluations to reach the optimum, 3$\times$ fewer than SA and 29$\times$ fewer than PSO. In Case~8 (Hyperbolic Fit), BFGS converges in only 294 evaluations (0.01\,s), demonstrating the exceptional efficiency of gradient-based methods on smooth, low-dimensional landscapes.

\textbf{SA occupies a middle ground}, with moderate computation times (1.6--6.2\,s) and a fixed evaluation budget of 16\,540 per case. The constant evaluation count reflects the predetermined cooling schedule: the algorithm always runs to completion regardless of whether convergence has been achieved. This represents a disadvantage compared to BFGS, which terminates adaptively once gradient norms fall below tolerance.

\textbf{PSO is the most expensive method}, requiring 150\,200 function evaluations per case (a fixed swarm size of 200 particles over 750 iterations). Despite this computational investment, PSO fails to produce feasible solutions in the majority of cases, yielding the worst cost-effectiveness ratio of the three methods. The high evaluation count is partly explained by the need to evaluate the entire swarm at each iteration, as opposed to the single-point evaluation strategy of BFGS and SA.

It should be noted that while the absolute timings are hardware-dependent and should not be compared across different machines, the relative ranking (BFGS $\ll$ SA $<$ PSO) and the function evaluation counts provide machine-independent performance metrics that are directly comparable.
