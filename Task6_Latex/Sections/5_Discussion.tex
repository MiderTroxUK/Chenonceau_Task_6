\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimisation}

\subsubsection{Convergence analysis}

Across all eight cases, the convergence curves exhibit the characteristic two‑phase behaviour commonly associated with Particle Swarm Optimisation: an initial rapid descent, driven by broad exploration, followed by a slower refinement period once the swarm approaches a promising region of the search space. Despite this shared structure, the rate and extent of convergence vary strongly from one case to another, indicating that each optimisation scenario presents a different level of landscape complexity. Cases 1, 3, 4, and 5 follow relatively long convergence trajectories, with improvements extending over several hundred iterations. Their curves suggest that the cost function contains wider or more intricate basins of attraction, requiring the swarm to progressively refine its position before reaching a stable minimum. In contrast, cases 2, 6, and 8 converge extremely rapidly, stabilising after only a few tens of iterations. This behaviour points to smoother or more accessible optima, where the swarm encounters a well‑defined descent direction early in the search. Case 7 sits between these two trends: it displays a strong initial drop comparable to the fastest‑converging cases, but continues to make gradual improvements up to around 200 iterations, indicating the presence of secondary features in the landscape that delay complete stabilisation. Overall, the variability in convergence rates reflects the heterogeneous nature of the underlying test cases. Rapidly converging cases seem to correspond to simpler or more convex search regions, while slowly converging ones point to more irregular or high‑dimensional surfaces where additional exploration is required. This diversity highlights the importance of assessing convergence behaviour individually rather than assuming uniform optimisation difficulty across cases.

\subsubsection{Profile analysis}

Taken together, the eight optimised profiles illustrate how the cooling‑tower geometry adapts to a wide spectrum of volumetric and boundary conditions. Despite all cases sharing the fundamental hyperbolic identity characteristic of natural‑draft cooling towers, the optimisation outcomes reveal four distinct geometric regimes, each driven by different balances between required height, waist radius, and curvature distribution. The tall and narrow‑waist towers (cases 3 and 6) demonstrate how the optimisation stretches the structure vertically when the available radius is constrained, producing pronounced upper flares to compensate for slender mid‑height sections. The medium‑height towers (cases 4 and 7) represent a more classical hyperbolic shape, where the curvature above and below the waist remains symmetric and structurally coherent. In contrast, cases 1 and 5 converge to almost identical mid‑height profiles, showing that certain target volumes admit a single dominant optimal geometry with limited room for variation. Finally, the most compact and wide‑waist shapes (cases 2 and 8) highlight scenarios where the optimisation requires minimal reshaping, as the volume can be satisfied with little deviation from a cylindrical‑like baseline. Across all regimes, the optimisation systematically redistributes curvature to satisfy the prescribed volume while maintaining smooth, physically meaningful tower contours. This diversity of outcomes reinforces the importance of analysing cooling‑tower profiles not only on the basis of their final area or error metrics but also in terms of their geometric structure, which provides valuable insight into the underlying constraints governing each optimisation scenario.

\subsubsection{Best result}

The optimal cooling tower configuration was selected based on the following criteria:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volume fidelity:}
    The target volume must be matched as closely as possible. Zero error is preferred; near‑zero is acceptable only if justified by other gains.

    \item \textbf{Convergence behaviour:}
    Fast, monotonic convergence to a stable plateau is required. Late oscillations or very long “creep” phases are penalised as they indicate sensitivity to stochastic effects or a poorly conditioned search landscape.

    \item \textbf{Area:}
    A smaller external envelope generally correlates with lower material use, reduced mass, and potentially lower cost. While structural verification is outside the present scope, area provides a consistent comparative surrogate at this stage.

    \item \textbf{Geometric plausibility of the cooling-tower profile:}
    The optimised profile should be smooth, physically meaningful, and free of abrupt kinks: a coherent hyperbolic silhouette, a reasonable waist, and balanced curvature above and below mid‑height.
\end{itemize}

Based on these criteria, case 8 emerges as the most appropriate cooling‑tower configuration. First, in terms of volume fidelity, case 8 achieves a zero volume error, indicating a perfect match with the prescribed target without requiring compensatory geometric distortions. This level of accuracy is only shared with a small subset of cases, yet case 8 distinguishes itself among them through superior performance on several other dimensions.
Regarding convergence behaviour, case 8 presents one of the cleanest and most rapid convergence histories. The cost function drops sharply within the first iterations and stabilises immediately, showing no signs of late oscillations or prolonged refinement phases. This stable behaviour provides confidence in the robustness of the obtained optimum and reduces the likelihood that the solution is sensitive to stochastic fluctuations. By contrast, some alternative cases with good volume fidelity exhibit longer convergence trajectories or more gradual plateauing, which weakens their comparative reliability.
The area measure also strongly supports the selection of case 8. It yields the smallest external envelope across all eight cases, significantly lower than those of the other zero‑error candidates. As area is used here as a proxy for material and fabrication efficiency, a reduced surface envelope implies a more economical and structurally lightweight design. This advantage is particularly striking when compared with cases 1 and 5, which, despite their excellent volume match, present substantially larger envelopes.
Finally, the geometric plausibility of the resulting cooling‑tower profile reinforces this choice. The optimised shape of  case 8 is smooth, coherent, and free of abrupt curvature transitions, exhibiting a compact silhouette with a consistent radial gradient and a physically realistic waist. Among all the profiles, it is one of the most regular and least distorted, requiring only modest adjustments from the initial guess while still achieving perfect volume compliance.
Taken together, these observations show that case 8 offers the best balance of accuracy, stability, efficiency, and physical credibility. It therefore represents the most defensible choice for the optimal cooling‑tower configuration within the scope of this study.

\subsection{Simulated Annealing}

\subsubsection{Convergence analysis}

The SA convergence histories across the eight cases exhibit the characteristic staircase descent pattern intrinsic to the Metropolis acceptance criterion: the objective remains constant during periods where no improving move is accepted, followed by abrupt drops when a beneficial perturbation is discovered. The most informative feature of these curves is the duration and depth of the initial exploration phase.

Cases~1, 4, and~7 display similar convergence dynamics: a rapid multi-order-of-magnitude decrease within the first 2\,000 iterations, followed by a long plateau with only marginal improvements. This suggests that the SA cooling schedule---and the associated step-size decay---drives the algorithm into a local basin early in the search, after which the temperature is too low to escape. The resulting solutions are nonetheless of good quality, with volume errors below 0.1\,m$^3$ and surface areas competitive with BFGS.

Cases~2 and~8 converge fastest, with the cost function stabilising after a single significant improvement step. In both cases, the low effective dimensionality of the search space (height-only optimisation for Case~2, three-parameter hyperboloid for Case~8) means that the SA neighbourhood structure efficiently samples the feasible manifold, and the algorithm encounters the basin of the global minimum early.

Case~3 (Full Freedom, $D = 19$) represents the most challenging scenario for SA. The higher dimensionality substantially expands the neighbourhood, diluting the probability that a random perturbation will yield a descent direction. Although the convergence curve shows a rapid initial decrease, the final surface area (10\,540\,m$^2$) is 36\% worse than BFGS, indicating that SA was unable to exploit the full parametric freedom.

Case~6 (High Volume) converges quickly but to a fundamentally flawed solution. The convergence curve itself appears healthy---rapid descent, stable plateau---yet the resulting profile is a physically unrealistic zigzag. This case illustrates a key limitation of SA: convergence of the objective function does not guarantee convergence to a physically meaningful solution when the cost function lacks an explicit smoothness penalty.

\subsubsection{Profile analysis}

The SA-optimised profiles fall into two distinct categories. For Cases~1, 2, 4, 5, 7, and~8, the resulting geometries are smooth, structurally coherent hyperboloids with well-defined waists between 18.5\,m and 24.2\,m. These profiles closely resemble the BFGS solutions, confirming that SA can reliably recover the correct geometric basin when the search space is sufficiently constrained.

Case~3 produces an elongated, narrow-waist tower (waist radius 11.1\,m) stretched to nearly 180\,m in height. While qualitatively hyperboloidal, this profile is more extreme than the BFGS counterpart, suggesting that SA's stochastic search settled in a different local minimum with unfavourable height--radius coupling.

Case~6 constitutes the most significant failure. The absence of a monotonicity or curvature penalty allows SA to generate a zigzag profile that, while satisfying the volume constraint, would be catastrophically unstable under wind loading. This observation has important engineering implications: for shape optimisation problems, penalty-based constraint handling alone is insufficient; explicit geometric regularity terms must be incorporated into the objective to ensure physical realism.

\subsubsection{Best result}

Among the SA results, Case~8 (Hyperbolic Fit) delivers the best performance on all four selection criteria. Its surface area of 5\,765.62\,m$^2$ is the lowest achieved by any algorithm--case combination except PSO on the same case, its volume error is exactly zero, its convergence is rapid and monotonic, and its profile is a smooth, compact hyperboloid. The parametric formulation eliminates the geometric irregularities that degrade SA performance in higher-dimensional cases, confirming that a well-chosen parametrisation is the single most effective lever for improving SA outcomes.

\subsection{BFGS}

\subsubsection{Convergence analysis}

The BFGS convergence curves are qualitatively distinct from those of both stochastic methods. Rather than the staircase pattern of SA or the two-phase descent of PSO, BFGS exhibits smooth, near-monotonic convergence with superlinear rates---a direct consequence of the progressively refined Hessian approximation. In Cases~1, 2, 4, and~6, the objective drops from $\mathcal{O}(10^{11})$ to $\mathcal{O}(10^{4})$ within 20--30 iterations, after which the gradient norm falls below tolerance and the algorithm terminates. This rapid convergence is characteristic of well-conditioned problems where the quadratic penalty term does not excessively distort the Hessian eigenspectrum.

Case~5 (Cylindrical Start) is noteworthy: despite the deliberately poor initialisation, BFGS converges within approximately 100 iterations, demonstrating the method's robustness to initial conditions when the search landscape is unimodal. The convergence curve shows a brief plateau around iterations 30--60, during which the inverse Hessian approximation accumulates sufficient curvature information to correctly orient the search direction away from the cylindrical basin.

Case~3 (Full Freedom, $D = 19$) reveals a fundamental limitation. The convergence curve shows a prolonged descent over hundreds of iterations, with the algorithm eventually terminating at a local minimum characterised by an extremely narrow waist (5.0\,m) and a volume error of 8.96\,m$^3$. This failure mode is directly attributable to the ill-conditioning introduced by simultaneously varying both radii and heights: the penalty term creates a narrow curved valley in the 19-dimensional search space, and the BFGS Hessian approximation---initialised as the identity---requires many iterations to capture the correct local curvature. In this regime, the method's local nature becomes a liability, as it cannot escape the basin of attraction it enters during the initial descent.

Case~8 represents the pinnacle of BFGS performance: convergence in 12 iterations (294 function evaluations) to a near-optimal solution, completed in 0.04\,s. This result confirms the theoretical expectation that BFGS achieves near-optimal efficiency when the search space is low-dimensional, smooth, and well-conditioned.

\subsubsection{Profile analysis}

The BFGS-optimised profiles are consistently the smoothest and most structurally coherent across all three methods. In Cases~1, 4, 5, and~7, the resulting hyperboloids exhibit symmetric curvature above and below the waist, with no oscillations, kinks, or abrupt transitions. This geometric regularity is inherent to gradient-based search: the local descent direction naturally favours smooth perturbations, as high-frequency oscillations produce large gradient magnitudes that are immediately corrected.

Case~2 (Variable Heights) is particularly instructive. BFGS redistributes the frustum heights non-uniformly, concentrating shorter sections near the waist and taller sections toward the extremities. This height redistribution achieves a 13\% area reduction compared to Case~1---a gain that SA fails to replicate (only 0.07\% improvement). This demonstrates that BFGS can exploit subtle inter-variable couplings that stochastic methods are unlikely to discover given their random perturbation strategies.

Case~6 (High Volume) starkly contrasts with SA: BFGS produces a smooth, physically realistic hyperboloid with an area of 7\,678\,m$^2$, whereas SA generates an unrealistic zigzag with an area of 36\,042\,m$^2$. This 4.7$\times$ difference underscores the natural advantage of gradient-based methods for shape optimisation: the local search inherently preserves geometric smoothness without requiring explicit regularisation terms.

\subsubsection{Best result}

BFGS achieves its best result on Case~8 (Hyperbolic Fit), with a surface area of 5\,961.77\,m$^2$, zero volume error, and the fastest computation time of all algorithm--case combinations (0.04\,s). Although the area is marginally higher than the SA result on the same case (5\,765.62\,m$^2$), the difference is small (3.4\%) and BFGS requires $56\times$ fewer function evaluations (294 vs.\ 16\,540). When considering the combined criteria of solution quality, constraint satisfaction, computational cost, and profile regularity, the BFGS--Case~8 combination represents the most efficient configuration in this study.

\subsection{Global discussion}

\subsubsection{Deterministic vs.\ stochastic trade-offs}

The results of this study confirm the classical trade-off between deterministic and stochastic optimisation methods, while revealing nuances specific to the cooling tower problem.

BFGS demonstrates unambiguous superiority in solution quality, constraint satisfaction, and computational efficiency for five out of eight cases (Cases~1, 2, 4, 5, and~6). Its gradient-based search naturally produces smooth, physically realistic profiles without requiring explicit geometric penalties. However, Case~3 exposes its Achilles' heel: in high-dimensional, ill-conditioned spaces, BFGS's local nature can trap it in suboptimal basins from which it cannot escape.

SA provides the best balance of exploration and constraint satisfaction among the stochastic methods. Its Metropolis acceptance criterion allows it to escape local minima during the high-temperature phase, while the cooling schedule ensures eventual convergence. The fixed evaluation budget of 16\,540 represents a reasonable computational investment, and volume errors remain below 0.5\,m$^3$ in all cases. However, the absence of gradient information means that SA cannot exploit inter-variable couplings (as demonstrated by its failure to replicate the BFGS height redistribution in Case~2) and cannot guarantee geometric smoothness (as demonstrated by the Case~6 zigzag).

PSO, in its current configuration, is the least suitable method for this problem class. The severe volume constraint violations in five out of eight cases indicate a fundamental mismatch between the penalty-based constraint handling and the swarm-based search dynamics. Unlike SA, which evaluates perturbations of a single solution and can therefore be efficiently guided by the penalty gradient, PSO distributes its evaluations across an entire swarm, diluting the penalty signal across many particles. For PSO to become competitive, its constraint handling would need to be redesigned---for example, by implementing a feasibility-preserving velocity update rule or a repair operator that projects infeasible particles onto the constraint surface.

\subsubsection{The role of problem formulation}

Perhaps the most significant finding of this study is that \textbf{the choice of problem formulation matters more than the choice of algorithm}. Case~8 (Hyperbolic Parametrisation, $D = 3$) yields the best results for all three methods, including PSO. By reducing the search space from 9--19 dimensions to just 3 parameters, the parametric formulation eliminates local minima, enforces geometric smoothness by construction, and enables even the weakest algorithm to converge to a near-optimal solution.

This observation has direct consequences for engineering practice: rather than investing effort in tuning algorithm hyperparameters or developing sophisticated constraint-handling strategies, the most effective approach is to select an appropriate parametrisation that embeds physical knowledge (smoothness, symmetry, monotonicity) directly into the search space. The hyperboloidal parametrisation achieves this by constraining the radial profile to a family of curves that are, by definition, smooth and structurally plausible.

\subsubsection{Practical engineering recommendations}

Based on the comparative results, the following algorithm selection guidelines emerge for cooling tower optimisation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{For well-constrained, low-dimensional problems} ($D \leq 10$, known bounds): BFGS is the method of choice, offering the fastest convergence and the highest-quality solutions with minimal tuning effort.
    
    \item \textbf{For high-dimensional or highly non-convex problems} ($D > 15$, disconnected feasible regions): SA provides a more robust search, provided that explicit geometric regularisation is incorporated into the cost function to prevent unrealistic profiles.
    
    \item \textbf{For parametric formulations} ($D \leq 5$, analytical profile models): all three methods converge effectively, and the choice can be driven by implementation convenience. BFGS remains the most efficient.
    
    \item \textbf{PSO is not recommended} for equality-constrained surface optimisation problems unless a problem-specific constraint-handling mechanism is implemented. The penalty-based approach is insufficient for the swarm dynamics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Limitations of the study}

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the penalty weight $\rho = 10^3$ was calibrated for BFGS and may not be optimal for the stochastic methods; a higher $\rho$ or an adaptive penalty schedule could improve PSO's constraint satisfaction. Second, the function evaluation budgets were not equalised across methods: PSO was allocated $29\times$ more evaluations than BFGS, yet still produced inferior results. A fairer comparison might fix the evaluation budget and compare solution quality at equal computational cost. Third, the study considers only a single cooling tower geometry class (frustum stack with fixed outer radii); generalisation to other structural forms (e.g., free-form shells, multi-material towers) would require further investigation. Finally, the analysis does not account for structural integrity: the surface-area-minimising profile may not satisfy stress, buckling, or wind-load criteria, which would need to be incorporated as additional constraints in a full engineering design workflow.
